Asymptotic Probability Theory, \(\sqrt{n}\) Variance Scaling, & Conditional Probability Updating

Part I — Modes of Convergence in Probability Theory

Unlike deterministic sequences of real numbers, a sequence of random variables \(\{X_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) defined on a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) consists of measurable functions \(X_n : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\). Because randomness introduces multiple ways to measure "closeness" to a limiting random variable \(X\), probability theory defines four primary modes of convergence.

1.1 Formal Definitions & Hierarchy

Mode of Convergence Notation Formal Mathematical Definition Role in Limit Theorems & Statistics
Almost Sure Convergence
Strongest Pointwise		 \(X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X\) $$P\left(\left\{\omega \in \Omega : \lim_{n \to \infty} X_n(\omega) = X(\omega)\right\}\right) = 1$$
Equivalently: \(\forall \varepsilon > 0,\; \lim_{n \to \infty} P\left(\sup_{k \ge n} |X_k - X| > \varepsilon\right) = 0\).		 Governs the Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN): almost every individual sample trajectory \(\bar{X}_n(\omega)\) permanently enters and stays inside any \(\varepsilon\)-tube around \(\mu\).
Mean-Square Convergence
\(L^2\) Norm		 \(X_n \xrightarrow{L^2} X\) $$\lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{E}\left[(X_n - X)^2\right] = 0$$
(Requires finite second moments: \(\mathbb{E}[X_n^2] < \infty\)).		 Fundamental in Mean Squared Error (MSE) estimation: an estimator \(\hat{\theta}_n\) converges in \(L^2\) to \(\theta\) iff \(\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}_n) \to 0\) and \(\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_n) \to 0\).
Convergence in Probability
Weak Convergence		 \(X_n \xrightarrow{P} X\) $$\forall \varepsilon > 0, \quad \lim_{n \to \infty} P\left(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon\right) = 0$$ Governs the Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN) and defines consistent estimators in statistics (\(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{P} \theta\)).
Convergence in Distribution
Weakest Mode		 \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X\) $$\lim_{n \to \infty} F_{X_n}(x) = F_X(x)$$
at every continuity point \(x\) of the limiting CDF \(F_X\).		 Governs the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) and asymptotic hypothesis testing (confidence intervals, \(z\)-tests, \(\chi^2\) goodness-of-fit).
Strict Implication Hierarchy:

$$\begin{array}{ccccc} X_n \xrightarrow{L^2} X & \Longrightarrow & X_n \xrightarrow{P} X & \Longrightarrow & X_n \xrightarrow{d} X \\ & & \Uparrow & & \\ & & X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X & & \end{array}$$ Note: Reverse implications fail in general. Neither \(L^2\) nor \(\text{a.s.}\) implies the other without extra conditions (e.g., uniform integrability or summable tail probabilities). However, if \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} c\) where \(c\) is a degenerate constant, then \(X_n \xrightarrow{P} c\).

1.2 Distinguishing Counterexamples (Why the Modes Differ)

  • In Probability, Not Almost Sure (The "Typewriter Sequence"): Define intervals \(I_1 = [0,1]\), \(I_2 = [0, 1/2]\), \(I_3 = [1/2, 1]\), \(I_4 = [0, 1/4]\), etc., on \(\Omega = [0,1]\) with uniform Lebesgue measure, and let \(X_n(\omega) = \mathbf{1}_{I_n}(\omega)\). Since \(\text{length}(I_n) \to 0\), \(P(|X_n - 0| > \varepsilon) = \text{length}(I_n) \to 0\), so \(X_n \xrightarrow{P} 0\) (and \(X_n \xrightarrow{L^2} 0\)). Yet for every \(\omega \in [0,1]\), \(X_n(\omega) = 1\) infinitely often and \(0\) infinitely often, so \(\lim_{n\to\infty} X_n(\omega)\) does not exist anywhere (\(X_n \not\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} 0\)).
  • Almost Sure, Not Mean-Square: Let \(X_n = n \cdot \mathbf{1}_{[0, 1/n]}\) on \([0,1]\). For every \(\omega > 0\), \(X_n(\omega) = 0\) once \(n > 1/\omega\), so \(X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} 0\). However, \(\mathbb{E}[X_n^2] = n^2 \cdot (1/n) = n \to \infty\), so \(X_n \not\xrightarrow{L^2} 0\).
  • In Distribution, Not in Probability: Let \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) and set \(X_n = -X\) for all \(n\). By symmetry, \(F_{X_n}(x) = F_X(x)\), so \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X\). Yet \(|X_n - X| = 2|X|\), which does not converge to \(0\) in probability.

1.3 Why \(\sqrt{n}\) Appears Ubiquitously in Convergence Theorems

The appearance of \(\sqrt{n}\) is a direct mathematical consequence of the additivity of variance under independence. Suppose \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n\) are i.i.d. random variables with mean \(\mathbb{E}[X_i] = \mu\) and finite variance \(\text{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2 \in (0, \infty)\).

1. Variance of the Partial Sum \(S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\):
Because pairwise covariances vanish under independence (\(\text{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = 0\) for \(i \neq j\)): $$\text{Var}(S_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n \text{Var}(X_i) + 2\sum_{1 \le i < j \le n} \text{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = n\sigma^2 \implies \text{SD}(S_n) = \sigma\sqrt{n}$$
2. Variance of the Sample Mean \(\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}S_n\) (LLN & Standard Error):
Using the quadratic scaling property \(\text{Var}(aY) = a^2\text{Var}(Y)\): $$\text{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{1}{n^2}\text{Var}(S_n) = \frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \implies \text{SE}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$
3. Unique Non-Degenerate Scaling for the CLT:
If we scale the centered sum \(S_n - n\mu\) by \(n^{-\alpha}\): $$\text{Var}\left(\frac{S_n - n\mu}{n^\alpha}\right) = \frac{n\sigma^2}{n^{2\alpha}} = \sigma^2 n^{1 - 2\alpha}$$
  • If \(\alpha > 1/2\) (e.g., \(\alpha = 1\) in LLN), variance \(\to 0\) (collapses to degenerate point mass at \(0\)).
  • If \(\alpha < 1/2\) (e.g., \(\alpha = 0\)), variance \(\to \infty\) (diverges to infinity).
  • Only at the critical exponent \(\alpha = 1/2\) (dividing by \(\sqrt{n}\)) does the variance stabilize to \(\sigma^2\), yielding \(\frac{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\).

Impact on Statistical Inference: Because standard error shrinks as \(\sigma / \sqrt{n}\), halving the width of a confidence interval or doubling estimation precision requires quadrupling (\(4\times\)) the sample size \(n\). If positive correlation exists among observations (\(\text{Cov}(X_i, X_j) > 0\)), variance grows faster than \(O(n)\), invalidating the standard \(1/\sqrt{n}\) rate.

Part II — Conditional Probability, Bayes' Theorem & Statistical Inference

2.1 Conditional Probability & Derivation of Bayes' Theorem

Given a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) and an event \(B \in \mathcal{F}\) with \(P(B) > 0\), the conditional probability of event \(A\) given \(B\) updates the sample space from \(\Omega\) to \(B\):

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \implies P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A)P(A) = P(A \mid B)P(B)$$

Combining the symmetry of intersection with the Law of Total Probability over a partition \(\{A_1, A_2, \dots, A_k\}\) of \(\Omega\) yields Bayes' Theorem:

$$P(A_i \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A_i) P(A_i)}{P(B)} = \frac{P(B \mid A_i) P(A_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^k P(B \mid A_j) P(A_j)}$$

2.2 The Three Core Pillars of Bayesian Statistical Inference

In Bayesian statistics, unknown model parameters \(\theta \in \Theta\) are treated as random variables rather than fixed constants. Observing empirical data \(D\) updates our uncertainty distribution over \(\theta\) via:

$$\underbrace{p(\theta \mid D)}_{\text{Posterior}} = \frac{\overbrace{p(D \mid \theta)}^{\text{Likelihood}} \cdot \overbrace{p(\theta)}^{\text{Prior}}}{\underbrace{\int_{\Theta} p(D \mid \theta') p(\theta')\, d\theta'}_{\text{Marginal Likelihood (Evidence) } p(D)}} \propto L(\theta \mid D) \cdot p(\theta)$$
Component Notation Role & Statistical Interpretation
Prior Probability \(p(\theta)\) Encodes domain knowledge, historical baseline rates, or structural beliefs about parameter \(\theta\) before observing current data \(D\).
Likelihood Function \(L(\theta \mid D) = p(D \mid \theta)\) Evaluates the probability of generating the observed data \(D\) as a function of \(\theta\). Acts as the data-driven mechanism reweighting hypotheses.
Posterior Probability \(p(\theta \mid D)\) The updated probability distribution over \(\theta\) after combining prior beliefs with empirical evidence. Serves as the new prior when future data arrives (sequential Bayesian updating).

2.3 Worked Examples: Prior, Likelihood & Posterior in Action

  • Example 1 — Intrusion Detection & The Base-Rate Fallacy (Discrete Case): Suppose a network experiences genuine zero-day attacks with prior probability \(P(A) = 0.005\) (0.5%). An IDS sensor has sensitivity (true positive likelihood) \(P(+ \mid A) = 0.98\) and false-positive rate \(P(+ \mid \neg A) = 0.04\). Upon triggering an alert (\(+\)), the posterior probability of an actual attack is: $$P(A \mid +) = \frac{0.98 \times 0.005}{(0.98 \times 0.005) + (0.04 \times 0.995)} = \frac{0.0049}{0.0049 + 0.0398} \approx 0.1096 \text{ (10.96%)}$$ Insight: Even with a 98% accurate detector, nearly 89% of alerts are false alarms due to the low prior base rate—a critical insight ignored by pure likelihood reasoning.
  • Example 2 — Conjugate Beta-Binomial Model for Vulnerability Rate \(\theta\) (Continuous Case): Suppose we model the unknown exploit success probability \(\theta \in [0, 1]\) using a conjugate \(\text{Beta}(\alpha_0, \beta_0)\) prior: \(p(\theta) \propto \theta^{\alpha_0 - 1}(1-\theta)^{\beta_0 - 1}\). After running \(n\) penetration trials with \(k\) breaches, the Binomial likelihood is \(L(\theta \mid k, n) \propto \theta^k (1-\theta)^{n-k}\). Multiplying prior and likelihood yields an analytical Beta posterior: $$\theta \mid (k, n) \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_0 + k,\; \beta_0 + n - k), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid D] = \frac{\alpha_0 + k}{\alpha_0 + \beta_0 + n}$$ Notice how the posterior mean is a precision-weighted compromise between the prior mean \(\frac{\alpha_0}{\alpha_0+\beta_0}\) and the sample MLE \(\frac{k}{n}\).

2.4 Advantages, Limitations & Influence on Statistical Conclusions

  • Advantages: Provides direct, intuitive probability statements about parameters (e.g., 95% credible intervals \(P(\theta \in [a,b] \mid D) = 0.95\)); naturally regularizes small-sample estimates against extreme noise; supports seamless sequential updating without discarding previous experiments.
  • Limitations: High-dimensional posteriors often lack closed-form integrals, requiring computationally intensive numerical sampling (Markov Chain Monte Carlo — MCMC, or Variational Inference); choice of prior introduces subjectivity when sample sizes are small.
  • Influence on Conclusions: When \(n\) is small, a strongly informative prior pulls the posterior toward prior expectations (shrinkage). As \(n \to \infty\), the likelihood dominates the prior (Bernstein–von Mises Theorem), so different reasonable priors converge to the same posterior centered at the true parameter.
