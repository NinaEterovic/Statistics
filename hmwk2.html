HW2 — ECC Toy Scheme, Caesar Cipher & Cryptanalysis

Comparative Analysis, Statistical Cryptanalysis, and Key-Space Vulnerabilities

1. ECC Toy Scheme vs. Caesar Cipher

ECC Toy Scheme: Built upon the elliptic curve model from Homework 1 (\(y^2 = x^3 + 7 \pmod{17}\) or restricted secp256k1 parameters). Encryption maps plaintext onto curve points or utilizes scalar multiplication for key agreement/ElGamal-like encryption, relying on the hardness of the ECDLP.

The Caesar Cipher: A classical substitution cipher shifting alphabet characters by a fixed key \(k \in [0, 25]\):

\(E(x) = (x + k) \pmod{26}, \quad D(x) = (x - k) \pmod{26}\)

Comparison: While ECC offers mathematical group-theoretic security and massive key spaces, the Caesar cipher has a tiny key space ($26$ possible keys) and preserves linguistic statistical properties in ciphertexts.

2. Statistical Cryptanalysis of the Caesar Cipher

By exploiting the known frequency distribution of letters in natural languages (e.g., English where 'E' and 'T' have highest frequency), an attacker can compute chi-squared ($\chi^2$) statistics or letter counts over ciphertexts of varying lengths:

  • Effectiveness: Highly effective on long ciphertexts where frequency distributions closely match theoretical language statistics.
  • Limitations: Extremely short ciphertexts lack sufficient statistical data, leading to false positives or ambiguous key recovery.

3. Restricted Key-Space Attack on ECC Toy Scheme

Because the toy scheme restricts the private key \(k\) to a tiny range (to make manual/computational experiments feasible), an attacker can easily execute a brute-force search:

\(\text{For each } k' \in [1, \text{Range}], \text{ compute } Q' = k' \cdot G \text{ until } Q' = Q\)

Why secure ECC is immune: Properly configured ECC schemes (like standard secp256k1) utilize a 256-bit private key space (\(\approx 2^{256}\)), making brute-force attacks computationally impossible even with all global computing power combined.
