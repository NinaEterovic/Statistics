HMWK 2 — ECC Encryption, Caesar Cipher & Cryptanalysis

Comparative Scheme Analysis, Statistical Attacks, and Key-Space Vulnerabilities

1. ECC Toy Scheme (secp256k1 Parameterized)

Using the elliptic-curve framework developed in Part 1 (based on restricted secp256k1 parameters / finite field arithmetic), we implement a simplified ElGamal-style or point-mapping encryption scheme.

  • Message Encoding: A simple message \(M\) is mapped onto an elliptic curve point \(P_m\).
  • Encryption Steps: 1. Sender chooses a random ephemeral scalar \(r\). 2. Computes the ciphertext pair \((C_1, C_2)\) where \(C_1 = r \cdot G\) and \(C_2 = P_m + r \cdot Q\) (with \(Q = k \cdot G\) as the recipient's public key).
  • Decryption Steps: The receiver uses their private scalar key \(k\) to recover the message point: \(P_m = C_2 - k \cdot C_1\), since \(k \cdot C_1 = k(r \cdot G) = r(k \cdot G) = r \cdot Q\).

2. The Caesar Cipher

As a classical baseline, the Caesar cipher encrypts letters by shifting them by a fixed integer key \(k \in [0, 25]\):

\(E_k(x) = (x + k) \pmod{26}, \quad D_k(x) = (x - k) \pmod{26}\)

The key acts as a direct shift offset across the English alphabet domain.

3. Comparative Analysis: ECC vs. Caesar Cipher

Feature ECC Toy Scheme Caesar Cipher
Mathematical Basis Elliptic-curve group laws over finite fields (ECDLP) Modular arithmetic shift substitution
Key Space Large algebraic domain (restricted in toy model) Extremely small (\(26\) possible keys)
Statistical Properties Ciphertext exhibits pseudo-random distribution; hides plaintext structure Preserves natural language letter frequency and n-gram patterns

4. Statistical Cryptanalysis of the Caesar Cipher

By analyzing a sufficiently large corpus of text in a known language, we exploit the non-uniform statistical distribution of letters (e.g., 'E', 'T', 'A' occurring with high frequency).

  • Algorithm Approach: Frequency matching or Chi-squared (\(\chi^2\)) scoring test across all 26 possible shift keys.
  • Effectiveness on Length: Highly effective on long ciphertexts where frequency distributions mirror language expectations. On very short ciphertexts, statistical variance causes false positives.

5. Restricted Key-Space Attack & Security Discussion

Because the toy model restricts the private key \(k\) to a small range to make manual verification feasible, an attacker can trivially recover \(k\) via brute-force search:

\(\text{Test all candidate keys } k' \text{ until } k' \cdot G = Q\)

Comparison with Caesar Attack: While the Caesar cipher is broken via statistical frequency distribution, the restricted ECC toy scheme is broken via brute-force algebraic search over a small scalar domain.

Why proper ECC is immune: A properly configured secp256k1 scheme utilizes a 256-bit key space (\(\approx 2^{256}\)), making brute-force search physically impossible due to astronomical computational complexity.
