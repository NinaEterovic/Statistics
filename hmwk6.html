HMWK 6 — Statistical Independence & Contingency Analysis

Bivariate Frequency Distributions, Expected Frequencies, \(\chi^2\) Hypothesis Testing & Causality

1. Theoretical Framework of Statistical Independence

In probability theory and statistics, two random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are statistically independent if knowing the outcome of one provides zero information about the probability distribution of the other.

1.1 Probability & Empirical Frequency Definitions

  • Joint Probability Factorization: For discrete random variables \(X\) and \(Y\), independence holds if and only if for every pair of values \((x_i, y_j)\): $$P(X = x_i \cap Y = y_j) = P(X = x_i) \cdot P(Y = y_j)$$
  • Conditional Probability Invariance: Equivalently, conditional distributions must equal the marginal distributions (provided \(P(X = x_i) > 0\)): $$P(Y = y_j \mid X = x_i) = \frac{P(X = x_i \cap Y = y_j)}{P(X = x_i)} = P(Y = y_j)$$
  • Empirical Contingency Table Formulation: Given a bivariate dataset of \(N\) observations categorized into \(r\) rows (levels of \(X\)) and \(c\) columns (levels of \(Y\)), let \(O_{ij}\) denote the observed frequency in cell \((i, j)\). The row marginal total is \(R_i = \sum_{j=1}^c O_{ij}\) and the column marginal total is \(C_j = \sum_{i=1}^r O_{ij}\).

1.2 Expected Frequencies & Pearson's Chi-Square (\(\chi^2\)) Test

Under the Null Hypothesis of Independence (\(H_0\)), the theoretical expected frequency \(E_{ij}\) for cell \((i, j)\) is derived from the product of marginal relative frequencies multiplied by total sample size \(N\):

$$E_{ij} = N \cdot \left(\frac{R_i}{N}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{C_j}{N}\right) = \frac{R_i \cdot C_j}{N}$$

To quantify deviations between observed data \(O_{ij}\) and theoretical independence \(E_{ij}\), we compute Pearson’s \(\chi^2\) test statistic, which asymptotically follows a \(\chi^2\) distribution with \(df = (r - 1)(c - 1)\) degrees of freedom:

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sum_{j=1}^{c} \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}, \qquad df = (r - 1)(c - 1)$$

To measure the normalized strength of association independent of sample size \(N\), we also compute Cramér's \(V\): \(V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{N \cdot \min(r-1, c-1)}}\), bounded in \([0, 1]\).

3. Interpretation: Statistical Association vs. Causality

Rejecting the null hypothesis \(H_0\) in a \(\chi^2\) test establishes statistical dependence (association), meaning the joint distribution of \((X, Y)\) systematically deviates from the product of their marginals. However, statistical dependence does not imply a causal mechanism (\(X \not\Rightarrow Y\)).

Why Association \(\neq\) Causation

  • Confounding Variables (Common Cause \(Z\)): A latent third variable \(Z\) may independently influence both \(X\) and \(Y\) (i.e., \(X \leftarrow Z \rightarrow Y\)). For example, high firewall alert volume (\(X\)) and increased server latency (\(Y\)) are strongly dependent, yet alerts do not cause latency—both are caused by an underlying DDoS traffic spike (\(Z\)).
  • Reverse Causality: A significant \(\chi^2\) statistic is symmetric with respect to \(X\) and \(Y\) (\(\chi^2(X,Y) = \chi^2(Y,X)\)). Mathematical dependence alone cannot distinguish whether \(X\) drives \(Y\) or \(Y\) drives \(X\).
  • Selection Bias & Collider Stratification: Sampling only compromised systems or conditioning on a common effect can induce artificial statistical dependence between two genuinely independent variables (Berkson's paradox).
  • Establishing Causality: Inferring causal direction requires experimental intervention (such as Randomized Controlled Trials / A/B testing), structural causal models (Pearl's do-calculus), or longitudinal temporal precedence rather than observational contingency tables alone.
